Yes. I’d design **Theirs** around one principle:

> **It should feel like visiting someone’s life, not visiting their obituary.**

If you get that right, you differentiate from almost every memorial-site competitor.

## 1. What a Theirs page actually is

A person gets one permanent page:

**`theirs.page/john-smith`**

That page becomes the home for their:

**photos · stories · milestones · voice · videos · relationships · memories contributed by others**

Not just:

> John Smith
> 1948–2026
> Obituary
> Condolences

That feels like a funeral website.

Instead, you're building a **life archive**.

---

# The actual memorial page

I'd structure every person's page approximately like this:

| Section                  | What it contains                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Opening**              | Portrait, name, birth/death years, short line about them            |
| **Their Story**          | Beautiful biography, written naturally rather than obituary format  |
| **Life Timeline**        | Childhood → school → marriage → children → work → important moments |
| **Memories**             | Individual stories contributed by family/friends                    |
| **Photos**               | Albums rather than one giant gallery                                |
| **People in Their Life** | Family/friends and relationship to the person                       |
| **Voice & Video**        | Recordings, old clips, speeches, messages                           |
| **Guestbook**            | Short condolences / messages / thoughts                             |
| **Legacy**               | Things they taught, sayings, recipes, traditions, achievements      |
| **About this memorial**  | Creator, contributors, privacy, last updated                        |

The page should have **one continuous storytelling experience**, not 15 dashboard-looking tabs.

# The hero should be extremely simple

Imagine:

**John Michael Carter**
*1948 — 2026*

> “He could fix almost anything, except his habit of telling the same joke twice.”

**142 memories · 387 photos · 26 contributors**

`Remember John` `Add a memory`

That immediately feels like **a person**.

Not a death record.

# The most important feature: Memories

I would actually make **Memories** the heart of the product.

Anyone invited can write:

> “I remember when Dad drove six hours because I told him my car wouldn't start…”

And attach:

* photo
* approximate year
* location
* people involved

Then the admin approves it.

Over time you aren't just getting an obituary.

You're assembling dozens/hundreds of tiny fragments of someone's life.

That is where the emotional value becomes enormous.

---

# Photos shouldn't just be a gallery

Your BringBack experience gives you an advantage here.

Instead of:

**Photos (287)**

allow families to organize them like:

**Childhood**
**School years**
**Wedding**
**Our family**
**Work**
**Travels**
**Things Dad loved**

And provide optional tools:

**Restore photo**
**Enhance photo**
**Remove scratches**
**Colorize**

But preserve the original permanently.

Never silently modify the historical photo.

# Life Timeline

This can become one of the signature features.

Example:

**1952**
Born in Jaipur

**1967**
Moved to Delhi with his family

**1974**
Married Meena

**1977**
Their first daughter was born

**1983**
Opened Sharma Hardware

**2006**
Became a grandfather

Photos and memories can attach to each point.

You eventually get a **visual biography** without the family having to write a 3,000-word biography.

# Collaborative by design

This product becomes much stronger when the creator doesn't have to build everything.

Memorial creator sends:

> **Help us remember Dad**

Then family gets a contribution page requiring no complicated account creation.

They can add:

**A memory**
**A photograph**
**A story about them**
**Something they used to say**
**A lesson they taught you**
**A recipe/tradition**
**Audio/video**

Admin approves before publication.

This also creates your viral loop.

One buyer can invite **20–100 relatives/friends**.

Those people now know Theirs exists.

# People in their life

This is something I'd make visually beautiful.

John's page might show:

**Meena — Wife**
**Rahul — Son**
**Anita — Daughter**
**Rohan — Grandson**
**David — Best friend**

And eventually those people can have their own Theirs pages.

Now something interesting happens:

You naturally start creating a **network of family histories**.

Not necessarily a genealogy product.

Just connections between real lives.

That can become powerful years later.

# Privacy should have exactly 3 modes

Don't complicate it.

| Mode         | Behaviour                               |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| **Public**   | Searchable on Google + shareable        |
| **Unlisted** | Anyone with link can visit, not indexed |
| **Private**  | Invitation/access required              |

And individual memories/photos should optionally be:

**Everyone / Family only / Admin only**

A public memorial might still contain private family photographs.

---

# Ownership model

Every page needs:

**Owner** → created memorial
**Co-admins** → trusted family members
**Contributors** → can submit content
**Visitors** → can view/comment depending on settings
**Successor** → takes control if owner can no longer manage it

That **Successor** field is important enough that I'd ask for it during onboarding.

> Who should take care of this page if you are unable to?

That's your long-term continuity problem partially solved at product level.

---

# Abuse/disputes

V1 should already include:

**Report page / Report memory / Report photo / Block contributor**

But don't let random reports automatically remove things.

Content states:

`submitted → pending → approved`

Admins control ordinary family disagreements.

**Theirs intervenes only for serious platform-policy issues**, impersonation, illegal content, harassment, privacy/IP claims, etc.

You should not become the referee deciding whose version of Grandpa's marriage is historically correct.

---

# Something competitors usually get wrong

Do **not** make the homepage creation flow:

> Create Memorial
> Name
> Birth Date
> Death Date
> Obituary

That's depressing and administrative.

I'd make yours:

### **Who would you like to remember?**

**Their name**

**Add a favourite photograph**

Then:

### Tell us a little about them.

Maybe:

**What did people call them?**
**What were they known for?**
**What did they love?**

Within two minutes they see a beautiful first version of the page.

Then progressively enrich it.

---

# AI should stay mostly invisible

Don't turn it into:

# AI MEMORIAL GENERATOR 🤖

Use AI behind features like:

**Turn answers into a biography**
**Organize memories into timeline**
**Suggest dates from photo metadata**
**Improve grammar without changing someone's voice**
**Create photo captions**
**Restore damaged photos**

Potentially:

> “Tell me about your mother in your own words.”

User writes five messy paragraphs.

The system turns that into a beautiful life story.

That's useful AI.

Dead-person chatbots and AI talking avatars should **not** be your core product.

---

# Preservation should become a feature, not legal fine print

Paid memorial:

### **Family Archive**

At any point:

**Download everything**

They receive:

`photos/`
`videos/`
`audio/`
`memories/`
`timeline.json`
`story.pdf`
`memorial.html`

So your promise isn't:

> Trust our startup to exist for 100 years.

It's:

> **Your family will never be trapped inside Theirs.**

That's an extremely strong trust message.

## And I'd make **one product decision that defines Theirs**

Other memorial products usually ask:

**“What information do we have about this deceased person?”**

Theirs should ask:

> **“What made this person them?”**

That difference should influence the entire UX.

Photos aren't files.
They're moments.

Timeline isn't dates.
It's their life.

Contributors aren't users.
They're people who knew them.

And the page isn't a memorial website.

### **It's their page.**

That is where **Theirs.page** suddenly becomes a very strong name:

> **Their photos. Their stories. Their life. Theirs.**

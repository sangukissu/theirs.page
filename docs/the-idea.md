# THEIRS — PRODUCT VISION

We are building **Theirs**, a place on the internet dedicated to a human life.

The product lives at **theirs.page**.

Do not think of this as a memorial website builder.

Do not think of it as an obituary platform.

Do not think of it as a photo gallery with a guestbook attached.

We are building a place where, after someone is gone, the people who knew them can slowly reconstruct the thing that death usually destroys first:

**the texture of who they were.**

A person's life is not their birth date and death date.

It is not a paragraph written for a funeral.

It is not a folder containing 423 JPEGs.

It is the stupid joke they repeated every Christmas.

The way they made tea.

The photograph where nobody was looking at the camera.

The nickname only their sister used.

The business they spent thirty years building.

The song they always played while driving.

The recipe nobody thought to write down.

A six-second video in which you can hear their laugh.

The advice they gave their daughter when she was nineteen.

The story five different people remember differently.

The chair they always sat in.

The places they lived.

The people they loved.

The ordinary things that seemed completely unimportant while they were alive, and suddenly become priceless when they are gone.

**That is what Theirs exists to preserve.**

---

# THE CORE IDEA

Every person gets a place that belongs to their story.

For example:

**theirs.page/robert-carter**

It should feel less like opening a web page and more like **entering someone's life**.

When someone arrives, the first thing they should understand should not be:

> Robert Carter died in 2026.

It should be:

> This was Robert.

The product should gradually reveal the person through photographs, stories, relationships, moments and voices.

Death explains **why the page exists**.

But life should dominate **what the page contains**.

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# THE EMOTIONAL JOB OF THE PRODUCT

People will usually create a page at one of the most emotionally chaotic moments of their lives.

They may be grieving.

They may have hundreds of photographs scattered across five phones.

Their brother may have the childhood pictures.

Their aunt may remember stories nobody else knows.

Someone has an old voicemail.

Someone else has the only video of their grandfather laughing.

Nobody has organized any of it.

And suddenly the family realizes:

**if we don't collect this now, pieces of this person are going to disappear.**

Theirs should remove that burden.

The product should quietly say:

> Start with what you have.
> You don't need to remember everything today.
> Other people can help.
> This page can grow over time.

Creating a page should therefore never feel like completing a government form about a deceased person.

It should feel like **beginning to remember them**.

---

# THE PAGE IS ABOUT A PERSON, NOT ABOUT CONTENT TYPES

This is an important design rule.

Do not think:

Photos section.
Biography section.
Timeline section.
Videos section.
Guestbook section.

Those are database concepts.

The visitor should experience **a person**.

A photograph might appear beside a story about that photograph.

A childhood milestone may contain the picture from that year.

A memory from a friend might appear within the period of life when they knew each other.

A quote might appear between two chapters of their story.

A voice recording may sit beside the story explaining why that recording matters.

The technology knows these are separate content objects.

**The visitor shouldn't feel the database.**

---

# MEMORIES ARE THE HEART OF THEIRS

The most valuable unit inside Theirs is not the photograph.

It is the **memory**.

A photograph can tell you what someone looked like.

A memory tells you **who they were**.

Imagine Robert's daughter writes:

> Dad couldn't walk past a broken appliance without trying to repair it. Once he spent half of Christmas Day fixing the neighbour's washing machine while everyone else was waiting for dinner.

That tiny story tells us more about Robert than a polished obituary ever could.

So Theirs should make contributing memories extraordinarily easy.

Someone visiting a page should be able to think:

**I remember something about him.**

And immediately contribute it.

The system can gently ask:

What happened?

When was this roughly?

Where were you?

Who else was there?

Do you have a photograph from that day?

But never turn storytelling into form-filling.

The person's imperfect words are often more valuable than perfectly polished prose.

---

# A MEMORIAL SHOULD BECOME RICHER WITH TIME

A conventional obituary is finished when it is published.

Theirs should work in the opposite direction.

A page may begin with:

one photograph,
a name,
three sentences.

Then somebody invites the family.

A cousin adds childhood photographs.

An old colleague adds a work story.

A daughter uploads a voicemail.

A grandson adds the recipe Grandpa taught him.

Someone identifies an unknown person in a photograph.

Another relative remembers the year the family moved cities.

Six months later the page contains parts of that life that **no single family member could have assembled alone**.

That collaborative accumulation is one of the most important ideas in the entire product.

Theirs isn't asking one grieving person to write someone's biography.

It allows **everyone who carried a piece of that person to bring their piece.**

---

# PHOTOS MUST HAVE CONTEXT

BringBack taught us something important.

Old photographs are valuable, but photographs without context slowly become anonymous.

A family finds an old photograph thirty years later and asks:

Who is standing beside Grandma?

Where was this taken?

What year was this?

Why was everyone there?

Eventually nobody knows.

Theirs should fight that loss.

A photograph can contain:

who is pictured,
approximately when it was taken,
where it happened,
which memory it belongs to,
who uploaded it,
the story behind it.

This transforms an image from **a file** into **family history**.

Photo restoration can become part of this experience later.

But restoration must always respect history.

Keep the original.

Never silently rewrite the past.

---

# THEIR LIFE STORY

Some families will have a beautifully written biography.

Most won't.

They will have fragments.

Dad grew up somewhere near Jaipur.

He moved when he was young.

He worked at a factory before opening his shop.

He met Mum through a cousin.

He loved cricket.

He hated flying.

He became completely different after his first granddaughter was born.

The product should help turn fragments into structure.

AI can assist here, but it should behave like **a thoughtful editor**, not an author inventing someone's life.

It can ask questions.

It can organize information.

It can clean grammar.

It can connect dates already supplied by the family.

It can suggest a narrative.

It must never fabricate memories because the page looks incomplete.

An incomplete true story is infinitely better than a beautiful false one.

---

# TIMELINE

The timeline shouldn't feel like Wikipedia.

It should feel like watching a life unfold.

1948.

Born.

1965.

The photograph from school.

1972.

The story about meeting his future wife.

1974.

Their wedding photograph.

1981.

The little shop he opened.

1998.

The family holiday everybody still talks about.

2007.

Holding his first grandchild.

Every milestone can contain people, photographs, stories and media.

Eventually the timeline becomes **a visual biography created collectively by the family**.

---

# PEOPLE MATTER AS MUCH AS EVENTS

Nobody's life exists alone.

Theirs should understand relationships.

Wife.

Brother.

Daughter.

Childhood friend.

Business partner.

Neighbour.

Grandchild.

Teacher.

A person's story is partly the collection of lives they touched.

Eventually this can form connections between pages.

Robert's wife may someday have her own Theirs page.

Their wedding photograph belongs meaningfully to both lives.

Their children connect those stories.

Over many years, Theirs could quietly become something much deeper:

**a human archive of interconnected family lives.**

Do not rush to build a genealogy product.

The relationship system should emerge naturally from remembering people.

---

# THE GUESTBOOK IS DIFFERENT FROM MEMORIES

Do not mix these concepts.

A guestbook entry might be:

> Thinking about you today. Miss you Uncle Robert.

A memory might be:

> Uncle Robert used to secretly give us ₹50 every time Dad wasn't looking...

Guestbook messages represent presence and emotion.

Memories become part of the historical record of the person's life.

Memories deserve richer treatment and stronger preservation.

---

# PRIVACY IS NOT AN AFTERTHOUGHT

Some families want the world to know someone's story.

Others want only twenty relatives to see it.

Both are valid.

The product must support:

**Public** — discoverable and indexable.

**Unlisted** — accessible through the link but not intended for search engines.

**Private** — only approved people can enter.

Privacy should feel simple and understandable.

Never make families decipher technical access-control language.

And a public page should still be able to contain individual items that remain family-only.

---

# THE FAMILY CONTROLS THE STORY

The creator of a page becomes its primary caretaker.

They can invite co-administrators.

Family and friends can contribute.

Contributions can require approval.

There must also be a successor administrator.

This matters because the person who creates a memorial today may themselves be gone thirty years from now.

The page cannot depend forever on one email address belonging to one human being.

Long-term stewardship must be designed into the product.

---

# FAMILY DISAGREEMENTS WILL HAPPEN

A person's life may contain divorce, estrangement, addiction, complicated family relationships, different recollections and conflicting perspectives.

Theirs must not attempt to become the ultimate authority on somebody's family history.

The page administrators curate the memorial.

The platform intervenes when necessary for issues such as impersonation, harassment, illegal material, privacy violations, fraud, intellectual-property claims or serious abuse.

The platform should not normally decide whether one sibling's memory of their father is more correct than another's.

Human memory is messy.

The product must understand that.

---

# TRUST IS THE REAL PRODUCT

Families are not simply uploading files.

They are handing us material that may be impossible to replace.

Therefore Theirs must behave differently from disposable consumer SaaS.

The user should be able to export their archive.

Original media should be preserved.

Important content should have backups.

Families should be able to appoint successors.

The system should avoid lock-in wherever practical.

We should never pretend that a startup can mathematically guarantee that a service will operate forever.

Instead, our philosophy is:

> **Your memories should not depend on Theirs existing forever.**

If a family can download the photographs, stories, metadata, recordings and a portable version of the memorial, then we have honored that principle.

---

# AI MUST REMAIN IN THE BACKGROUND

Do not make Theirs look like an AI product.

Nobody comes here because they want to use AI.

They come because they miss someone.

AI may help:

organize scattered information, identify timeline gaps, improve badly written text without erasing the contributor's voice, transcribe audio, help caption photographs, restore damaged images, organize large collections, or ask thoughtful questions that uncover forgotten stories.

But AI should never become the emotional protagonist.

And Theirs should never casually generate fake words, fake memories or fake historical facts attributed to a real person.

Authenticity is sacred here.

---

# DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

The product should feel warm, calm, intimate and human.

Not gloomy.

Not funeral-black.

Not angel wings, candles, clouds and generic grief imagery.

Not aggressively cheerful either.

Think:

a beautifully made family album,
a quiet documentary,
a carefully preserved box of letters,
a home filled with photographs.

Whitespace should make content breathe.

Typography should favor reading.

Photographs should be large enough to matter.

Motion should be subtle.

The interface should disappear when someone is reading a memory.

We are not trying to impress the visitor with UI.

We are trying to make them **forget that they're using software.**

---

# CREATION EXPERIENCE

Never begin with:

> Create Memorial
> Date of Death
> Funeral Information
> Obituary

Instead begin with:

### Who would you like to remember?

Then:

**What was their name?**

**What did everyone call them?**

**Add a photograph you love.**

Then perhaps:

> Tell us one thing that immediately comes to mind when you think about them.

Within minutes, there should already be something beautiful on the screen.

The creator should feel:

**Yes. That's them.**

Then the product can gradually ask for more.

---

# WHAT WE MUST NEVER BECOME

These are non-negotiable:

* We are **not** an obituary database.
* We are **not** a funeral-home website template.
* We are **not** Dropbox for dead people's photographs.
* We are **not** an AI chatbot pretending to be somebody who died.
* We are **not** trying to manufacture grief or engagement.
* We do not use emotional manipulation to sell storage.
* We do not make people feel that failing to upgrade means losing someone.
* We do not invent history to make a memorial prettier.
* We do not lock a family's only copy of their memories inside our platform.
* We do not measure product success primarily by how long grieving people stay on the website.

The product wins when a family looks at a page and says:

> **“That's him.”**

---

# THE NORTH STAR

Imagine a child born today.

Their grandfather died before they were old enough to remember him.

Twenty years later they visit his page.

They don't just see:

**Rajesh Sharma
1958–2026**

They see him young.

They hear his voice.

They read their mother's story about the day he taught her to ride a bicycle.

They see the shop he built.

They discover the joke everyone says he told too often.

They see photographs of him holding them when they were a baby.

They read what his friends remembered about him.

And for fifteen minutes, this person who existed before their own memories becomes **real**.

That is the product.

Not hosting.

Not profiles.

Not memorial pages.

### We are trying to prevent a human life from slowly collapsing into a name, two dates, and a box of unidentified photographs.

**Theirs exists so that a person can remain knowable.**

Their face.

Their voice.

Their stories.

Their people.

Their life.

**Theirs.**
